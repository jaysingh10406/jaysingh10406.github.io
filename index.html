Case study — rebuilding an engineering foundation

I joined an early-stage energy startup as Director of Software Engineering in October 2025. The company had no source control. Containers were mounted onto ECS by hand. The AWS account was controlled by an outside vendor, who also held roughly half the engineering work. Nobody could say with confidence what was running in production or why it behaved the way it did.

The order below is the part I'd want to be judged on. Each stage is a precondition for the next one: you can't run root cause analysis on a system you can't see, you can't safely change infrastructure without a pipeline, and you can't have a pipeline without source control. Nobody handed me that sequence.

  1. 01

    Source control

    The organization had none. I established a Git workflow.

    Every change became reviewable, attributable, and reversible.

  2. 02

    Account custody

    The AWS account sat with an outside vendor. I brought it under direct company control.

    We could change our own infrastructure without asking permission.

  3. 03

    Audit of what we'd inherited

    Catalogued outdated dependencies and misconfigurations across the codebase and infrastructure, then remediated them.

    A known starting state instead of an assumed one.

  4. 04

    Delivery

    Replaced manual container mounting with a CI/CD pipeline across dev, staging, and production.

    Shipping became a repeatable process rather than a manual event.

  5. 05

    Security controls

    Led the engineering work for a SOC 2 audit — single sign-on, SAST, dependency scanning, and vulnerability remediation, all built and run in-house.

    A security posture we could evidence rather than assert.

  6. 06

    Observability

    Instrumentation and alerting on a system that had neither.

    Root cause analysis grounded in evidence instead of recollection.

  7. 07

    Removing classes of failure

    Once enough incidents had been analyzed properly, the more useful pattern showed up: the same architectural causes were surfacing as different symptoms. I changed the infrastructure to remove those classes of failure rather than keep closing the tickets they produced.

    Engineering time moved from repair toward prevention.

On the pipeline in stage four

An outside vendor quoted roughly 600 hours for it. I judged that the number described the vendor's position rather than the problem, and built it in about a week using Claude Code. The pipeline is the receipt. The decision was declining the quote.

Alongside this I absorbed roughly half the engineering work the vendor had been doing, coordinated two vendor engineering teams, and trained three new hires. Eleven months from an organization with no version control to one closing architectural defects it found through its own telemetry.

About

The pattern above isn't specific to that company. At Apple I spent three years leading a team accountable for commerce paths a vendor understood better than we did. At Meta I ran training for 40 engineers and managers across two regions, where the knowledge that mattered lived in people's heads rather than anywhere durable. Different surfaces, same problem: a team on the hook for outcomes it couldn't fully control.

So when I inherit a system, I look at custody before anything else — who can change this, who can see it, who owns the account it runs in. Architecture and code quality reviews are worth very little while the answer to those questions is "someone else." I write production code across backend, frontend, and infrastructure, mostly in Python, Node.js, React, and AWS.
